Equations, numerical method, assumptions & a classroom guide

1. The thermodynamics of a spherical nucleus

All calculations use SI units. The fusion enthalpy ΔHf and diffusion activation energy QD are entered in kJ mol⁻¹ and converted to J mol⁻¹. Vm is entered in cm³ mol⁻¹. A positive Δg denotes the free-energy decrease on solidification. Equal solid and liquid atomic volumes are assumed.

Δgm = ΔHf(1 − T/Tm)
Δgv = Δgm / Vm  ;  Ω = Vm / NA
Δμ = Δgm / NA
ΔG(r) = 4πγr² − (4π/3)r³Δgv
r* = 2γ / Δgv
ΔG* = 16πγ³ / (3Δgv²)
n* = (4π/3)r*³ / Ω

The driving-force approximation holds fusion enthalpy and entropy constant and neglects heat-capacity differences. The interfacial free energy γ is isotropic and constant. The continuum capillarity approximation is questionable for very small n*.

2. A steady-state CNT nucleation rate

D(T) = D₀ exp[−QD / (RT)]
Z = [Δμ / (6πkBTn*)]1/2
f⁺ = 24D(n*)2/3 / λ²
J = Ω−1 Z f⁺ exp[−ΔG* / (kBT)]

Z is the Zeldovich factor, f⁺ is an attachment frequency, and λ is the atomic jump distance. The prefactor is an explicit diffusion-based closure, not a universal constant. J is the rate of successful nuclei per unit remaining parent volume. Barrier, rate and waiting time are distinct quantities. References [1–2] describe these CNT expressions.

3. A separate postcritical growth law

u(T) = (6D/λ){1 − exp[−Δgm / (RT)]}
rᵢ(t) = 1.05r* + u(T)(t − tᵢ)

This is a planar Wilson–Frenkel-type law with interlayer spacing d = λ and incorporation fraction f = 1 [3]. CNT itself does not prescribe a complete growth law. Using the planar velocity immediately after nucleation is an approximation: curvature-dependent early growth is not resolved. No solute or thermal diffusion field is solved; “diffusion-based” here refers to the kinetic prefactor.

4. Random events, three-dimensional geometry

A periodic cube of side L has volume V = L³. Homogeneous Poisson candidate events have rate JV over the entire cube. Their continuous times and uniform 3D positions are sampled with a seeded pseudorandom generator. A candidate is rejected when its centre has already transformed; this thinning gives rate J Vparent. Birth acceptance uses continuous sphere geometry, not screen pixels or volume sampling points.

Δtcandidate = −ln(U) / (JV)
P(no first nucleus by t) = exp(−JVt)
Mean first-nucleus waiting time = 1/(JV)

Accepted nuclei start at 1.05r*, with no artificial minimum visible size. Equal isotropic growth speeds permit an extended-sphere, first-arrival construction: the union of growing spheres locates the transformed region, and the first front to reach a point determines its grain identity. Grain identities are not overwritten at impingement. The finite birth sphere is a postcritical insertion approximation; only its centre is tested for parent-phase membership.

The main image is a 320 × 320 raster section, not a projection. A 40 × 40 × 40 midpoint quadrature estimates the volume fraction. The rotatable cube shows birth-position markers, not scaled grain radii. A grain can exist in the cube without intersecting the displayed section. Opposite faces are periodic, so a grain can appear at opposite image edges.

5. Transformation kinetics and numerical limits

XJMAK(t) = 1 − exp[−(π/3) J u³ t⁴]

This reference assumes a statistically infinite 3D material, steady homogeneous nucleation, zero initial seed radius and constant isotropic growth. A single finite periodic sample fluctuates about that limit; inserted test seeds and finite birth radii violate its assumptions. The reference is hidden after a manual seed is inserted.

A frame processes a bounded number of candidates to preserve browser responsiveness. When limited, the physical clock advances less rather than dropping events or altering J or u. The run explicitly pauses at 2,000 retained nuclei. It also pauses once the volume estimate reaches 99.9%; unresolved residual liquid can remain. Rates outside floating-point range retain logarithmic readouts but can be inactive on the numerical clock. The volume quadrature and image resolution require convergence checks for quantitative use.

6. Try these experiments

Start at 540 K: press Run, observe nucleation, growth and impingement, then move the section slider. Restart with seed 17 to repeat the realization. Change γ or ΔHf and observe the barrier and waiting time.

Compare temperatures at the same clock speed: near Tm, spontaneous nucleation can be effectively absent. Insert a labelled test seed to see how growth differs from nucleation. Deep undercooling can suppress both kinetic rates. Fit clock accelerates the display and reports the new timescale; it is not a physical acceleration.

Change D₀: in this model both J and u scale linearly with D at fixed T. This rescales the clock but does not independently control grain density. Increasing D₀ alone should reproduce the same stochastic geometry at the same transformed fraction, with all event times rescaled.

7. Scope

An isothermal ideal bath removes released latent heat. There are no heterogeneous nucleants, elastic strain, anisotropy, dendrites, solute partitioning, heat flow, coarsening, transient nucleation time lag, or glass-transition model. At T ≥ Tm, forward solidification is disabled; melting is not modelled. Defaults are illustrative, and no quantitative prediction for a named material is implied.
