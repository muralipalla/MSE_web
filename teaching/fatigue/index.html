Why study a load the part has already carried?

A rotating axle bends under the weight it supports. A surface point passes from compression to tension and back on each turn. Repeated local slip can initiate a crack near a notch, scratch or inclusion, even when the overall load is below the part’s static capacity.

Once a crack starts, further cycles can grow it until the remaining section fractures. Knowing the stress history and the current crack size helps determine when to inspect or replace the part. Find the fatigue origins on a fractured propeller →

The same amplitude can act around a different mean

In the general stress-cycle graph, compare −80 to 80 MPa with −60 to 100 MPa. Both cycles have an amplitude of 80 MPa. The second has a 20 MPa tensile mean, which can reduce life. The Goodman correction estimates an equivalent fully reversed amplitude using the ultimate tensile strength Su.

σa,eq = σa / (1 − σm/Su)

80 MPa amplitude, 20 MPa tensile mean and Su = 490 MPa give 83.4 MPa on an R = −1 curve. Surface residual stress also shifts the local mean and can relax in service.

A service shift contains many amplitudes

Rainflow counting groups cycles in an irregular record by range and mean. A repeating record of 0 → 140 → 60 → 100 → 0 MPa contains an inner 60–100 MPa cycle and an outer 0–140 MPa cycle. Each is read against its own fatigue life.

D = Σ ni/Ni; repeated blocks to D = 1 are 1/D.

Miner’s rule divides each bin’s count ni by its constant-amplitude life Ni. Identify the bin that consumes the most life. Investigate sequence effects when overloads change the local response.

What changes when the material yields every cycle?

A vibration can produce many mostly elastic cycles, characteristic of high-cycle fatigue. A constrained detail may experience fewer cycles with appreciable plastic strain, characteristic of low-cycle fatigue. The local response determines the transition.

After plastic tension, the yield interval shifts toward tension. Compressive yielding begins closer to zero. This is the Bauschinger effect.

How to read the cycles

The initial load takes the specimen from zero to the tensile strain peak. Each counted cycle then goes to the compressive strain peak and back. The first reversal shows the asymmetry most clearly. With kinematic hardening, the backstress reverses as the loading reverses, and a later loop under symmetric strain can be nearly symmetric.

This illustrative model uses E = 200 GPa and virgin yield limits of ±250 MPa. Isotropic hardening expands the interval with accumulated plastic strain. Kinematic hardening shifts its centre with signed plastic strain. Both use the same shifted power law g(x) = (1 + x/0.002)0.4 − 1. At the prescribed strain rate, a cubic overstress law brings plastic flow in gradually.

The area enclosed by a closed loop is the mechanical work dissipated per unit volume in that cycle. The elastic strain amplitude is σa/E. The plastic strain amplitude is the total strain amplitude minus the elastic part.

Use both strain components to predict life

Δε/2 = (σ′f/E)(2Nf)b + ε′f(2Nf)c

Coffin–Manson–Basquin fits the elastic and plastic amplitudes separately. Solve for cycles Nf from the local total mechanical strain amplitude. The fit uses two reversals per full cycle. With tensile mean stress, one option is a calibrated Smith–Watson–Topper model based on the product of peak tensile stress and strain amplitude.

Translate the stress cycle into ΔK

ΔK = Y(a) Δσ √(πa)

For a wide plate with a central through-crack, a is half the crack length and Y is about one. A growing crack raises ΔK even if the applied stress cycle stays the same.

Growth is slow near the long-crack threshold. Short cracks can grow at lower applied ranges. Fit Paris’s law, da/dN = C(ΔK)m, over the intermediate region at the appropriate stress ratio. Near fracture, growth accelerates as peak intensity approaches the applicable toughness.

Mode I stress intensity · Paris and Erdogan

Count the cycles from one crack size to the next

Ngrow = ∫a₀aₗᵢₘ da / (C[ΔK(a)]m)

Methods to improve fatigue life

Lower the stress or strain at the critical location, make it harder for a crack to start, and account for cracks that can still grow in service.

Reduce the cyclic load at the hot spot

Reduce vibration or resonance, stiffen the part where appropriate, and improve the load path to lower the local stress amplitude. Reducing a tensile mean stress can also reduce the severity of the cycle. Use local strain–life data when starts, stops or overloads cause plastic strain.

Round notches and protect the surface

Use smooth fillets and gradual section changes, deburr holes, and remove machining scratches where fatigue cracks are likely to start. Reduce pitting and fretting by protecting exposed surfaces and controlling relative motion at joints.

NASA analysis of flaws at fillets · NASA case of a corrosion pit at a fillet

Introduce compressive residual stress

Shot peening, laser peening, or cold expansion of a hole can leave the treated region in compression. This reduces the local tensile stress that opens small surface cracks. Check how much compression remains after the expected temperature and load history.

NASA tests of shot and laser peening

Control defects and plan inspections

Choose a material, heat treatment, and manufacturing route with fatigue data for the expected loading and environment. Control pores, inclusions, and weld defects at critical locations. Use the crack-growth model to estimate the cycles from a detectable flaw to the assessment size and set inspection intervals.

What does a 20% reduction in cyclic stress range do? In the Paris regime, at the same crack size and geometry, it reduces ΔK by 20%. For an illustrative Paris exponent of 3, the growth rate falls to 0.8³ ≈ 0.51 of its original value at that crack size.

Summary and formulas

Use stress–life for predominantly elastic cycles, strain–life when local plastic strain matters, and crack-growth analysis when you know the starting flaw size.

Describe the stress cycle

Δσ = σmax − σmin, σa = Δσ/2

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2, R = σmin/σmax, T = 1/f

M = Fℓ/2, σa = 32M/(πd³)

In the rotating-beam test, the total hanging force F is shared by the two collars, ℓ is the lever arm, and d is the round gauge diameter. One revolution gives one cycle at zero mean stress and R = −1.

Estimate stress–life and cumulative damage

σa = A Nfb

Fit Basquin’s relation over the finite-life part of the S–N curve. A runout establishes a lower bound on life.

σa,eq = σa/(1 − σm/Su), D = Σ ni/Ni

The Goodman correction uses ultimate tensile strength Su to estimate a fully reversed equivalent amplitude for a tensile mean. Miner’s rule adds the damage from ni cycles in each bin with test life Ni. A repeated block with damage D reaches the model’s failure criterion after about 1/D blocks.

Use the local strain amplitude

Δε/2 = (σ′f/E)(2Nf)b + ε′f(2Nf)c

In Coffin–Manson–Basquin, the first term is elastic and the second is plastic. Each cycle contains two reversals. Choose coefficients for the material and use the local mechanical strain range.

εa,e = σa/E, εa,p = Δε/2 − εa,e

Use the hysteresis loop to read the stress amplitude and separate the strain components.

Integrate growth from the measured crack

ΔK = Y(a) Δσ √(πa), da/dN = C(ΔK)m

Ngrow = ∫a₀aₗᵢₘ da / {C[ΔK(a)]m}

Choose a Paris fit for the material and stress ratio. Integrate only over the fit range, from the measured size to the assessment limit. In the central through-crack example, a is half the crack length and Y is about one.
