Slip & Schmid’s Law

A tensile load produces normal and shear stresses on inclined crystal planes. Use the crystal orientation to calculate these stresses and compare the conditions for slip and cleavage.

Explore the specimen ↓

From tension to slip

One plane, one direction

A slip system consists of a crystallographic slip plane and a slip direction lying in that plane. A uniaxial tensile stress σ produces a shear stress resolved along that direction. Under Schmid’s onset criterion, slip begins when the magnitude of this resolved shear stress reaches the critical value for the system.

Schmid’s law

τRSS = σ cos φ cos λ

m = |cos φ cos λ|

φ is the angle between the tensile axis and the plane normal. λ is the angle between the tensile axis and the slip direction. m is the non-negative Schmid factor.

Two components of traction

Normal stress and shear stress on a plane

Resolved normal stress acts perpendicular to a chosen plane. With tensile stress taken as positive, it tends to open that plane. Shear traction acts within the plane. Under uniaxial tensile stress σ, at angle φ between the tensile axis and plane normal,

σn = σ cos² φ

One cosine comes from resolving the force; the other comes from the inclined area. For an applied tensile force F and a section crossing the bar with 0 ≤ φ < 90°, let A⊥ be the area perpendicular to the load and Aφ the inclined area, both measured in the same configuration. Then σ = F/A⊥, Fₙ = F cos φ, Aφ = A⊥/cos φ, and σₙ = Fₙ/Aφ = (F/A⊥) cos² φ.

The magnitude of the total in-plane shear traction is

τplane = σ |sin φ cos φ|

τRSS is the projection of this shear traction along a particular crystallographic slip direction. It can be zero even when τplane > 0. The normal stress depends on the plane orientation, independently of the slip direction chosen within it.

A plane perpendicular to the tensile axis has φ = 0: σₙ = σ and zero shear. The total in-plane shear reaches its maximum σ/2 at φ = 45°. Kelly, normal and shear traction

01 / Geometry

What the factor means

The factor m measures the effectiveness of the applied tensile stress in producing shear on a particular slip system. For perpendicular plane normal and slip direction, 0 ≤ m ≤ 0.5. The maximum occurs when both angles are 45°.

In three dimensions φ and λ do not generally add to 90°. The slip direction must remain in the plane, so the two angles cannot be selected independently without respecting that constraint.

02 / Slip onset

Critical resolved shear stress

τCRSS is the magnitude of resolved shear stress required to initiate slip on a particular system under the specified conditions. It depends on material, temperature, strain rate, and microstructure.

For the chosen system, onset is predicted when |τRSS| ≥ τCRSS. For m > 0, the corresponding tensile stress is σyield = τCRSS/m. If m = 0, this loading produces no resolved shear on that system.

Worked example

Predict the tensile stress for slip

A single crystal is pulled in tension. For one slip system, the plane normal makes 45° with the tensile axis and the slip direction also makes 45° with it. The critical resolved shear stress is 30 MPa. Find the Schmid factor and tensile stress at slip onset for this system.

  1. Calculate the orientation factor: m = cos 45° cos 45° = 0.5.
  2. Apply the critical condition: σyield = τCRSS/m = 30/0.5 = 60 MPa.
  3. At 60 MPa, |τRSS| = 60 × 0.5 = 30 MPa, the specified critical value.

This predicts onset on the specified system. Under this idealized criterion, compare τCRSS/m across the available systems with m > 0 to find which reaches onset first. The crystal’s slip families provide those candidate systems.

Slip systems in crystals

To apply the worked example to a particular crystal, identify its available plane–direction pairs. Dislocations glide on the slip plane, and their Burgers vector b is parallel to the slip direction. Crystal structure determines the available pairs.

Parentheses (hkl) identify a particular plane, and square brackets [uvw] identify a particular direction. Braces {hkl} and angle brackets ⟨uvw⟩ denote families of symmetry-related planes and directions. Hexagonal crystals commonly use four indices, (hkil) and [uvtw], with i = −(h + k) and t = −(u + v). An overbar denotes a negative index. For HCP, determine plane normals and angles using the hexagonal lattice geometry.

In HCP, ⟨a⟩ slip has a Burgers vector in the basal plane, while ⟨c+a⟩ slip has both basal and c-axis components.

FCC

Aluminium, copper, and nickel commonly slip on their close-packed {111} planes along close-packed ⟨110⟩ directions. Four plane orientations, each containing three slip directions, give 4 × 3 = 12 systems.

BCC

Ferritic iron, molybdenum, and tungsten have ⟨111⟩ slip directions. The three plane families listed give 48 geometrical candidates. Which systems carry slip depends on their resistance, temperature, strain rate, and loading orientation. Other stress components can also affect screw-dislocation glide in BCC metals, producing departures from the simple Schmid onset criterion. BCC dislocation study

HCP

Magnesium, zinc, and α-titanium have hexagonal structures. Basal ⟨a⟩ slip produces no normal plastic strain along the c axis. Pyramidal ⟨c+a⟩ slip can accommodate this strain. The activity of basal, prismatic, and pyramidal systems, and of deformation twinning, depends on the material and test conditions.

For each permitted plane–direction pair, the resolved shear stress is τRSS = σ cos φ cos λ. The angles describe that particular system. In the Schmid onset model, assess its resolved shear stress against its own critical resolved shear stress. Activation depends on orientation and resistance under the test conditions.

From crystal indices to stresses

Resolve stress from Miller indices

Specify the cylinder’s tensile axis relative to the crystal axes. Express the tensile axis, plane normal, and slip direction in the same crystal frame before calculating their angles.

A calculation route for cubic crystals

Let the tensile axis be [UVW], the slip plane be (hkl), and the slip direction be [uvw]. For a cubic crystal, the plane normal is parallel to [hkl]. Normalize the index triples to obtain unit vectors:

L̂ = (U,V,W)/√(U² + V² + W²), n̂ = (h,k,l)/√(h² + k² + l²), d̂ = (u,v,w)/√(u² + v² + w²)

  1. Check that the direction lies in the plane. In this cubic calculation, require hu + kv + lw = 0.
  2. Calculate the two direction cosines.

    cos φ = L̂ · n̂ = (Uh + Vk + Wl)/[√(U² + V² + W²) √(h² + k² + l²)]

    cos λ = L̂ · d̂ = (Uu + Vv + Ww)/[√(U² + V² + W²) √(u² + v² + w²)]

  3. Resolve the applied tensile stress. Use τRSS = σ cos φ cos λ, m = |cos φ cos λ|, and σn = σ cos² φ. The shear sign indicates slip sense; compare its magnitude with the CRSS.

These component formulas use the orthogonal, equal-length cubic axes. HCP directions and plane normals require the hexagonal lattice metric; do not normalize their four-index tuples as Cartesian vectors.

Worked example 1

A cylinder loaded along [112]

An FCC single-crystal cylinder has its tensile axis parallel to [112]. The applied tensile stress is σ = 180 MPa. Assume the crystal remains elastic at this load. Compare the resolved shear and normal stresses for the three plane–direction pairs below.

FCC directions in the ⟨110⟩ family can be slip directions on {111} planes when they satisfy the in-plane condition. On (111), that condition becomes u + v + w = 0. Both [110] and [101] satisfy it.

  1. Find the loading direction and the first plane normal.

    L̂ = (1,1,2)/√6, n̂(111) = (1,1,1)/√3, cos φ = (1 + 1 + 2)/(√6 √3) = 4/√18

    Thus both systems on (111) have σn = 180(4/√18)² = 160 MPa.

  2. Resolve along the requested direction. For system A, (111)[110], the loading-axis dot product is −1 + 1 + 0 = 0. Hence cos λ = 0, λ = 90°, and τRSS = 0.
  3. Choose a second direction in the same plane. For system B, (111)[101], the dot product is −1 + 0 + 2 = 1, giving

    cos λ = 1/√12, mB = 2/(3√6) ≈ 0.2722, |τB| ≈ 49.0 MPa

  4. Compare a different plane. System C is (111)[011]. Its in-plane check is 0 − 1 + 1 = 0. The loading-axis dot products are 1 − 1 + 2 = 2 for the normal and 0 + 1 + 2 = 3 for the slip direction:

    mC = (2/√18)(3/√12) = 1/√6 ≈ 0.4082, |τC| ≈ 73.5 MPa

    The normal stress is σn,C = 180(2/√18)² = 40.0 MPa.

The next examples add material thresholds. For cleavage, evaluate normal stress on the candidate cleavage plane, which can differ from the slip plane.

Compare two initiation criteria

Slip or cleavage: which starts first?

Slip responds to resolved shear along a slip direction. Cleavage opens a crack across a crystallographic plane. In a simple critical-normal-stress model, cleavage is predicted to initiate on a candidate plane when its resolved tensile normal stress reaches a prescribed threshold σcleav. This is an effective strength for stated material and flaw conditions. It is distinct from an ideal bond-breaking strength or a fracture-toughness value.

Let ψ be the angle between the tensile axis and a cleavage-plane normal. On that plane, σn,c = σ cos² ψ. Compare the applied tensile stresses needed to reach the two criteria, using every candidate system and plane. Here i labels slip systems and j labels candidate cleavage planes:

σapp,slip = mini: mᵢ > 0(τCRSS,i/mᵢ)

σapp,cleav = minj: cos² ψⱼ > 0(σcleav,j/cos² ψⱼ)

The smaller applied threshold identifies the first criterion reached in this elastic comparison. Zero orientation factors give no finite threshold on that system or plane under this loading.

In ferrite, {100} is the predominant cleavage family. The examples below use that family in a hypothetical BCC model, with equal τCRSS = 100 MPa for all 48 listed slip systems and σcleav = 180 MPa on every {100} plane. These are teaching inputs, not measured properties of a named alloy. Ferritic-steel cleavage-plane study

The model compares initial events at fixed orientation under uniform uniaxial stress and uses Schmid’s slip criterion. A slip-first result denotes initial yielding. Subsequent cleavage depends on the evolving local stresses, flaws, constraint, and plastic deformation; the later elastic threshold is not a post-yield fracture prediction. Real BCC non-Schmid effects are outside this example. Microstructure and critical cleavage stress

    Along [001], the (001) normal is parallel to the load, so cos² ψ = 1 and the cleavage criterion is reached at 180/1 = 180 MPa.

    Along [011], L̂ = (0,1,1)/√2. The (010) and (001) planes both have cos² ψ = 1/2, so their cleavage threshold is 180/(1/2) = 360 MPa.

  2. Compare the criteria.

At 180 MPa along [001], cleavage reaches its threshold while the largest resolved shear is only 180(√2/3) = 84.85 MPa, below the 100 MPa CRSS. Along [011], slip starts at 212.13 MPa while the largest cleavage-plane normal stress is 212.13/2 = 106.07 MPa, below the 180 MPa cleavage threshold.

Orientation changes the competition. The best slip factor is the same for these two axes, but their cleavage-plane normal factors differ. This is why comparing only the Schmid factors would miss the change in the first event.

Change slip resistance, keep the axis

Keep the tensile axis [001] and the hypothetical {100} cleavage threshold at σcleav = 180 MPa. Compare equal CRSS values of 60 MPa and 100 MPa on all 48 slip systems. Geometry is unchanged, so mmax = √2/3.

For the 60 MPa CRSS case, slip starts while the cleavage-plane normal stress is σn,c = 127.28 MPa, below the 180 MPa cleavage threshold.

The crossover occurs when the two applied thresholds are equal:

τCRSS* = mmax σcleav = (√2/3)(180) = 84.85 MPa

Below this model CRSS, slip begins first; above it, cleavage reaches its criterion first. At equality, the scalar criteria are reached together, and this model does not resolve their subsequent interaction.

Increasing slip resistance can therefore favour cleavage in the competition. Temperature and strain rate can alter the resistance to slip in BCC metals. Here the cleavage threshold was deliberately held fixed to isolate that effect; these inputs do not predict a numerical ductile-to-brittle transition temperature.
