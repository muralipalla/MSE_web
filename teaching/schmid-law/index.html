Slip & Schmid’s Law

A tensile force can drive shear on an inclined crystal plane. The plane orientation and slip direction determine the resolved shear stress that drives dislocation glide.

Explore the specimen ↓

From tension to slip

One plane, one direction

A slip system consists of a crystallographic slip plane and a slip direction lying in that plane. A uniaxial tensile stress σ produces a shear stress resolved along that direction. Under Schmid’s onset criterion, slip begins when the magnitude of this resolved shear stress reaches the critical value for the system.

Schmid’s law

τRSS = σ cos φ cos λ

m = |cos φ cos λ|

φ is the angle between the tensile axis and the plane normal. λ is the angle between the tensile axis and the slip direction. m is the non-negative Schmid factor.

01 / Geometry

What the factor means

The factor m measures the effectiveness of the applied tensile stress in producing shear on a particular slip system. For perpendicular plane normal and slip direction, 0 ≤ m ≤ 0.5. The maximum occurs when both angles are 45°.

In three dimensions φ and λ do not generally add to 90°. The slip direction must remain in the plane, so the two angles cannot be selected independently without respecting that constraint.

02 / Slip onset

Critical resolved shear stress

τCRSS is the magnitude of resolved shear stress required to initiate slip on a particular system under the specified conditions. It depends on material, temperature, strain rate, and microstructure.

For the chosen system, onset is predicted when |τRSS| ≥ τCRSS. For m > 0, the corresponding tensile stress is σyield = τCRSS/m. If m = 0, this loading produces no resolved shear on that system.

Worked example

Predict the tensile stress for slip

A single crystal is pulled in tension. For one slip system, the plane normal makes 45° with the tensile axis and the slip direction also makes 45° with it. The critical resolved shear stress is 30 MPa. Find the Schmid factor and tensile stress at slip onset for this system.

  1. Calculate the orientation factor: m = cos 45° cos 45° = 0.5.
  2. Apply the critical condition: σyield = τCRSS/m = 30/0.5 = 60 MPa.
  3. At 60 MPa, |τRSS| = 60 × 0.5 = 30 MPa, the specified critical value.

This predicts onset on the specified system. Under this idealized criterion, compare τCRSS/m across the available systems with m > 0 to find which reaches onset first. The crystal’s slip families provide those candidate systems.

Slip systems in crystals

To apply the worked example to a particular crystal, identify its available plane–direction pairs. Dislocations glide on the slip plane, and their Burgers vector b is parallel to the slip direction. Crystal structure determines the available pairs.

Parentheses (hkl) identify a particular plane, and square brackets [uvw] identify a particular direction. Braces {hkl} and angle brackets ⟨uvw⟩ denote families of symmetry-related planes and directions. Hexagonal crystals commonly use four indices, (hkil) and [uvtw], with i = −(h + k) and t = −(u + v). An overbar denotes a negative index. For HCP, determine plane normals and angles using the hexagonal lattice geometry.

In HCP, ⟨a⟩ slip has a Burgers vector in the basal plane, while ⟨c+a⟩ slip has both basal and c-axis components.

FCC

Aluminium, copper, and nickel commonly slip on their close-packed {111} planes along close-packed ⟨110⟩ directions. Four plane orientations, each containing three slip directions, give 4 × 3 = 12 systems.

BCC

Ferritic iron, molybdenum, and tungsten have ⟨111⟩ slip directions. The three plane families listed give 48 geometrical candidates. Which systems carry slip depends on their resistance, temperature, strain rate, and loading orientation. Other stress components can also affect screw-dislocation glide in BCC metals, producing departures from the simple Schmid onset criterion. BCC dislocation study

HCP

Magnesium, zinc, and α-titanium have hexagonal structures. Basal ⟨a⟩ slip produces no normal plastic strain along the c axis. Pyramidal ⟨c+a⟩ slip can accommodate this strain. The activity of basal, prismatic, and pyramidal systems, and of deformation twinning, depends on the material and test conditions.

For each permitted plane–direction pair, the resolved shear stress is τRSS = σ cos φ cos λ. The angles describe that particular system. In the Schmid onset model, assess its resolved shear stress against its own critical resolved shear stress. Activation depends on orientation and resistance under the test conditions.
