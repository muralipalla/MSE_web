Model convention

Start from one consistent bond law

The supplied graph has a well depth of 1 eV, so this lesson uses the normalized Mie form U(x) = Cε[(σ/x)m − (σ/x)n]. For the default 12–6 potential, C = 4 and the minimum is exactly −ε.

The tensile model below converts a single-bond holding force into an interplanar traction using the tutorial assumption ρb = 1/x02. The model is deliberately ideal: every bond is identical and the crystal contains no defects.

The important question

Why do real materials have far inferior strength than perfect crystals?

The ideal calculations require every bond across a plane to resist together. Real crystals have easier, highly localized routes for deformation and fracture.

Perfect crystal

Every atom would cross the energy barrier coherently.

↓far less applied stress
Real crystal

A dislocation advances the registry locally, a few bonds at a time.

Reveal the mechanisms that lower real strength
Dislocations

Glide moves a small core region instead of shearing an entire plane at once.

Cracks and notches

Stress concentrates at a sharp tip, so local bonds see far more stress than the nominal value.

Interfaces and surfaces

Grain boundaries and free surfaces supply easier sites for slip, damage, and crack nucleation.

Thermal activation

Atomic fluctuations help defects cross barriers before the ideal athermal stress is reached.

Defects do not always weaken a material: solutes, precipitates, grain boundaries, and other dislocations can obstruct glide and raise the yield strength. Near-perfect nanoscale crystals can approach ideal strength.
