Creep

How does a hot turbine blade keep its shape?

A jet engine’s gas can be hotter than the melting point of its turbine blade alloy. Cooling air and a ceramic thermal barrier keep the blade metal cooler. Even below melting, a spinning blade experiences sustained tensile stress. Over many hot hours it can slowly lengthen and close the small gap to the surrounding casing. This time-dependent deformation is creep.

Some high-pressure turbine blades are cast as single crystals of a nickel-based superalloy. Removing grain boundaries removes pathways for boundary diffusion and sliding. The material and the cooling system work together to keep creep slow enough for the blade to retain its shape in service.

Use the curve to predict life ↓

Two ways to run out of life

In primary creep the strain rate often falls. The shallowest part gives the minimum rate, even when there is little steady plateau. In tertiary creep the rate rises as the section thins or damage builds. Use the slope for a deformation estimate and the failure time for rupture.

Time to a strain limit

The furnace’s dashed guide sits 0.5% strain above the elastic jump. Clearance or dimensional tolerance may set a limit like this. For nearly steady creep, divide the remaining strain allowance by the estimated rate. At 0.002%/h, a 0.30% allowance lasts about 150 h.

ε̇min = Aσn exp[−Q/(RgT)]

The Norton–Arrhenius fit gives the stress exponent n from the slope against log stress and the activation energy Q from the slope against inverse temperature in kelvin. Use the full creep curve when primary or tertiary strain matters.

Time to rupture

The red dot on the reference curve marks rupture. Test several stresses and temperatures to estimate its location under other conditions. One approach fits stress against the Larson–Miller parameter, using an absolute temperature and a constant fitted to the material.

PLM = T[CLM + log10(tr/1 h)]

Use kelvin for T and hours for the rupture time tr. Once you have a fit, find the parameter at the service stress and use the service temperature to recover the rupture time.

Worked example at 250 MPa and 650 °C

  1. Read the parameter. Start at 250 MPa on the stress axis, move across to the dashed fit, and read PLM/1,000 ≈ 22.7035 on the horizontal axis. The fitting tests span 300 to 700 MPa, so this step extrapolates in stress.
  2. Convert back to time. At 650 °C, T = 923.15 K. Substitute PLM ≈ 22,703.5 K into the rearranged equation.

tr = 1 h × 1022,703.5/923.15 − 20 ≈ 39,200 h

Compare with the held-out test at 100 MPa and 750 °C. Farther along the dashed line the fit predicts about 8,360 h, while the specimen ruptured after 1,595.2 h. This is why checking extrapolations against tests near the intended service conditions matters.

A turbine run contains more than one test condition

Return to the rotor-rim log. During each start the peak thermal stress adds to the centrifugal load. The longer rated dwell runs cooler. Split the log into periods with similar metal temperature and stress, then read a rate and rupture time for each.

Add the strain increments

Δεc ≈ Σ ε̇i Δti

Multiply each creep rate by the hours at that condition. If inspection gives permanent elongation, use it to update the strain budget. If the section thins, recalculate stress for the remaining material.

Add the fractions of rupture life

Dc = Σ Δti/tr,i

Robinson’s rule compares the exposure in each period with its constant-condition rupture life. The model reaches rupture at a total of one. The linear sum assumes the order of the periods leaves the rates and rupture properties unchanged.

We can now compare the two limits for the 1,000-hour log. Both use the same duty cycle and material data.

Why would a different microstructure shift the curves?

Homologous temperature T/Tm compares the operating and melting temperatures in kelvin. Temperature promotes atomic transport. Stress, grain size and microstructure help determine how that transport produces deformation.

Coble creep

Atoms diffuse along grain boundaries toward the faces pulled apart by tension. The grains elongate along the tensile axis.

Model and interpretation

Each field shows the highest of four relative creep rates. The model uses a fixed shear modulus G, a reference grain size d0 of 5 µm, and illustrative coefficients. The Alloy 718 furnace uses a separate material fit.

log₁₀ rᵢ = aᵢ + nᵢ log₁₀(σ/G) − qᵢ(Tₘ/T − 1/0.68) − pᵢ log₁₀(d/d₀)

Doubling grain size at fixed temperature and stress reduces the modeled Coble rate by a factor of eight and the Nabarro–Herring and fine-grained sliding rates by a factor of four. The dislocation-creep law has no grain-size term in this model, so its field grows as these competing rates fall. The map extends the fine-grained sliding law over the whole slider range for comparison. Sliding can also accommodate deformation in other fields.

Diffusional creep

Stress drives atomic transport between grain faces. Lattice diffusion controls Nabarro–Herring creep, and grain-boundary diffusion controls Coble creep. Smaller grains shorten diffusion distances and can raise the rate in these regimes.

Review diffusion →

Dislocation creep

Diffusion of vacancies enables edge dislocations to climb out of their slip planes and bypass obstacles. Climb and subsequent glide can control creep under higher stress. Precipitates and their stability matter.

Review dislocation motion →

Boundary damage

Grains can slide relative to each other while diffusion or dislocations accommodate the shape changes. Cavities can develop at boundaries under tensile stress and join to form a crack. The rupture life also depends on the strain the material can sustain.

Lower creep rate can accompany shorter rupture life

At 980 °C and 110 MPa, tests of a directionally solidified nickel superalloy gave a minimum rate of 0.0061%/h and a mean rupture time of 794 h for transverse specimens. Longitudinal specimens gave 0.0086%/h and 1,456 h. The transverse specimens had lower elongation to fracture.

Equiaxed cast blades have grain boundaries in many orientations. Directional solidification reduces boundaries transverse to the main stress. How grain-boundary cavities become cracks

During prolonged tensile creep, tiny voids can nucleate at grain boundaries, often near particles or junctions where local stresses concentrate. Cavities grow by diffusion and deformation of the surrounding metal. Neighboring cavities can join into a crack that follows the grain boundary.

Methods to improve creep life

Lower the metal temperature

Use cooling, insulation or thermal barrier coatings as the application allows. On turbine blades, internal cooling passages and thermal barrier coatings lower the metal temperature. The exponential temperature term in the creep-rate law explains why even a modest reduction can matter.

Rolls-Royce on how turbine blades stay cool

Reduce sustained tensile stress

Increase the load-bearing section where practical, share the load across more of the part, and use smooth transitions at attachments. Lower local stress reduces the creep rate and helps delay strain accumulation and rupture.

Choose a microstructure that stays stable

Use an alloy and heat treatment that retain strengthening precipitates or dispersoids over the service exposure. Larger grains can help where diffusional creep dominates. Directional solidification aligns elongated grains with the main tensile load, reducing the number of boundaries across it. Single-crystal blades eliminate grain boundaries.

Frost and Ashby on creep mechanisms · NASA single-crystal creep tests

Limit the hottest operating periods

Shorten peak-temperature holds where the process permits, and track how long the part spends at each hot condition. Use the service-history lab to see how a hotter dwell changes accumulated strain and consumed rupture life.

Try a 10 °C reduction in the furnace. At an initial stress of 450 MPa, lowering the metal temperature from 650 to 640 °C cuts the fitted minimum creep rate from about 2.51 × 10−4 to 1.29 × 10−4 %/h. A hot dwell changes a fatigue cycle

Load hold and fixed-strain hold

Under sustained load, creep strain accumulates. Under a fixed total strain at constant temperature, creep strain grows by taking up part of the elastic strain, so the stress relaxes. In a simple uniaxial model the relaxation rate is approximately −E times the creep strain rate.

Use the changing stress during a constrained hold when estimating the creep fraction. Using the starting peak for the entire hold can misrepresent the exposure.

Compare cycles with and without a dwell

A startup and shutdown can impose a thermal strain cycle. Vibration can add many smaller cycles during running, and the hot dwell adds creep exposure. Compare strain-controlled test loops at the same strain range with and without a tensile hold. Relaxation during the hold and the changed lifetime reveal creep–fatigue interaction.

Use dwell-fatigue data to assess this interaction where it matters. Compare life in starts and in hot hours, with the startup rate and dwell duration stated.

Summary and formulas

Read a creep test for its strain rate and time to rupture. For a changing service history, estimate both the time to the strain limit and the time to rupture. The earlier limit governs.

Read a constant-load test

σ0 = F/A0, ε(t) = [L(t) − L0]/L0

εe ≈ σ0/E(T), εc(t) = ε(t) − εe

In the furnace test, measure the initial cross-section A0 and gauge length L0 at test temperature before loading. Subtract the elastic jump to read creep strain. The slope falls in primary creep, reaches a minimum, then rises in tertiary creep toward rupture. True stress F/A(t) can rise as the section thins under the fixed force F.

Estimate the time to a strain limit

ε̇min = Aσn exp[−Q/(RgT)]

trem,ε ≈ (εc,lim − εc,now)/ε̇min

The Norton–Arrhenius law uses material parameters A and n, activation energy Q, and the gas constant Rg. Use kelvin for T. The time estimate assumes a nearly steady rate at fixed conditions. Use consistent strain units for the allowance and rate.

Recover rupture life from an LMP fit

PLM = T[CLM + log10(tr/1 h)]

tr = (1 h) × 10PLM/T − CLM

Read PLM at the service stress from the stress–LMP graph. Convert the plotted value from thousands of kelvin before substituting. Use the same material constant CLM as the fit, and check extrapolations against independent rupture tests.

Add creep strain and consumed rupture life

Δεc ≈ Σ ε̇i Δti

Dc = Σ Δti/tr,i, Dc = 1 at modeled rupture

Split the service log into periods with similar metal temperature and stress. For each period, use its own creep rate and constant-condition rupture time tr,i. Robinson’s rule adds the life fractions and assumes that their order leaves the rupture properties unchanged.

Compare mechanisms on the map

θ = T/Tm, s = σ/G

ε̇i ∝ d−pi at fixed T and σ

Use absolute melting temperature Tm, shear modulus G, and grain size d. On the illustrative map, equal modeled rates define field boundaries. The grain-size exponents are 3 for Coble creep, 2 for Nabarro–Herring creep and fine-grained boundary sliding, and 0 for the dislocation-creep law.

Read stress relaxation during a dwell

ε̇tot = 0 ⇒ σ̇ ≈ −Eε̇c

During a fixed-strain hold at constant temperature, increasing creep strain reduces elastic strain and relaxes the tensile stress. This relation uses uniaxial loading and a constant elastic modulus E. Use the evolving stress when estimating creep during the hold.
