Uniaxial
Tension Test

A tensile test measures how a specimen deforms as a machine pulls it along one axis. Use the force and gauge extension to identify elastic response, yielding, strain hardening, and necking in a ductile metal.

01 / Tensile measurements

Stress and strain

The load cell measures tensile force F. An extensometer measures extension between two points on the specimen that define its gauge length. Axial stress is force per cross-sectional area. Axial strain measures the relative change in length.

Axial tensile stress

Stress has units of pressure. In metals testing, MPa is convenient because 1 N/mm2 = 1 MPa.

Engineering stress

σE = F / A0

Divide force by the original cross-sectional area A0 of the gauge section.

True stress

σT = F / A

Divide force by the current cross-sectional area A to obtain the mean axial stress across that section.

Axial tensile strain

Strain is dimensionless. A strain of 0.10 can also be reported as 10%.

Engineering strain

εE = (L − L0) / L0

Divide the extension by the original gauge length L0.

True (logarithmic) strain

dεT = dL / L  →  εT = ln(L / L0)

Accumulate increments of extension relative to the current length L. Gauge-length and local axial strain coincide when the gauge deforms uniformly.

Converting the measurements. For the same marked length, εT = ln(1 + εE) follows directly from the definitions. For uniform deformation with approximately constant gauge volume (a useful approximation during plastic flow in dense metals), AL ≈ A0L0, so σT ≈ σE(1 + εE).

Example. With F = 6.00 kN, A0 = 20.0 mm2, measured A = 18.2 mm2, and a gauge length increasing from 50.0 to 55.0 mm, the stresses are 300 MPa (engineering) and about 330 MPa (true). The strains are 0.100 (engineering) and about 0.0953 (true).

02 / Onset of plastic deformation

Yield stress and proof stress

Yielding is the onset of plastic deformation. In a uniaxial test, the initial linear slope of the stress–strain curve gives Young’s modulus E. A clear yield point and a gradual transition call for different ways of reporting yield strength.

Distinct yield points

Some low-carbon steels exhibit discontinuous yielding. The upper yield strength σy, upper is the engineering stress at the initial peak before the first yield drop. The lower yield strength σy, lower is the lowest engineering stress during the yielding stage, excluding any initial transient after the drop.

During the yield plateau, plastic deformation spreads along the gauge section in bands. The stress then rises as the specimen strain hardens.

0.2% offset proof stress

When the transition is gradual, draw a line parallel to the initial elastic line, starting at 0.2% engineering strain (0.002 as a fraction) on the strain axis. The engineering stress at its intersection with the measured curve is the 0.2% proof strength σpy, 0.2.

The offset defines a specified plastic extension using the elastic slope. Plastic deformation may begin below σpy, 0.2.

03 / Loading and unloading

Elastic and plastic parts of total strain

In the small-strain elastic–plastic model, unloading to zero force recovers the elastic part. The remaining extension gives the plastic part.

Why 45°? Under uniaxial stress σ, the shear stress resolved along a slip direction is τR = σ cos φ cos λ, where φ and λ are the angles between the tensile axis and, respectively, the slip-plane normal and the slip direction. Its maximum magnitude is σ/2, attained when the two angles are 45°. In a real crystal, slip begins on a crystallographic slip system when its critical resolved shear stress is reached. 05 / Engineering stress–strain curve

Yield points, resilience, and toughness

The two plots show the same illustrative tensile test. The yield detail zooms in on the elastic region and yield plateau. Both plots use linear axes.

The shaded triangle ends at the elastic limit in the yield detail.

Modulus of resilience

The recoverable elastic energy per unit volume is the area under the curve up to the elastic limit. For linear elasticity, Ur = σel2/(2E), where σel is the stress at the elastic limit.

This model places the elastic limit at upper yield. Here E = 200 GPa and σel = 260 MPa, so Ur = 0.169 MJ/m3.

Modulus of toughness

The entire area under the engineering curve to fracture, ∫0εf σE dεE, is the tensile work per unit original gauge volume. It is about 120 MJ/m3 for the piecewise linear curve shown.

Use strain as a fraction in the integral. An area of 1 MPa on a fractional-strain axis equals 1 MJ/m3. Once deformation localizes, the work per original gauge volume can depend on the gauge length. Fracture toughness measures a material’s resistance to crack extension.

06 / Uniform plastic flow

Power-law hardening

During strain hardening, more true stress is needed to continue plastic flow. In many metals, dislocation interactions contribute to this increase in flow stress.

Hollomon equation

σT = K(εT)n

Assumption. This form uses total true strain εT and neglects elastic deformation.

The strength coefficient K has units of stress, and the strain-hardening exponent n is dimensionless. Fit the law over a suitable range of uniform plastic flow, after any yield plateau and before necking. Convert engineering test data to true stress and true strain before fitting.

On logarithmic axes, plot true stress against true strain. The fitted slope is n. Use strain as a fraction when calculating K.

Example. If true stress increases from 400 to 500 MPa as true strain increases from 0.05 to 0.15, the two-point estimate is n = ln(500/400)/ln(0.15/0.05) ≈ 0.203. Then K = 400/(0.050.203) ≈ 735 MPa. For measured data, fit all points in the selected strain range.

07 / Localization

Onset of necking

During uniform plastic elongation, the gauge section gets thinner as it gets longer. The tensile force F = σTA still rises while strain hardening offsets that loss of area. A diffuse neck starts to form when the uniform section can no longer sustain an increase in force.

Considère criterion

Assume rate-independent plastic flow, uniform deformation up to the onset of necking, and approximately constant gauge volume. Then dA/A ≈ −dεT. At maximum load, dF = A dσT + σT dA = 0, which gives the criterion:

dσT/dεT = σT

For a Hollomon material with negligible elastic strain, this gives εT,neck ≈ n. For example, n = 0.20 predicts about 0.20 true strain at the end of uniform elongation, or e0.20 − 1 ≈ 22.1% engineering strain.

After necking. Deformation concentrates in the neck. Gauge-length strain describes the overall extension, while a local strain measurement is needed to resolve deformation in the neck. Dividing force by the measured minimum area gives the mean axial stress on that section. The neck has a multiaxial stress state, so this average alone is insufficient to recover the uniaxial flow curve.

08 / Maximum load

Ultimate tensile strength

Ultimate tensile strength (UTS), σTS, is the engineering stress corresponding to the maximum tensile force Fmax. Calculate it using the original cross-sectional area A0.

σTS = Fmax / A0

For a typical ductile metal, this peak marks the onset of diffuse necking. As the force falls after the peak, engineering stress falls with it. The engineering fracture stress uses the force just before separation and is lower on such a curve. For a brittle specimen that fractures without necking, maximum force can occur at fracture.

For a specimen that yields discontinuously and then strain hardens, use the maximum force after the yield plateau to calculate σTS. In the illustrative curve, σTS = 460 MPa, and the engineering fracture stress is 305 MPa.

Worked problem

The true stress–strain curve of a material follows σT = 500εT0.3, where σT is in MPa and εT is expressed as a fraction. Find the ultimate tensile strength. Assume the specimen deforms uniformly at constant volume up to the onset of necking.

Solution

  1. Find the true strain at maximum force. By the Considère criterion, dσT/dεT = σT at the onset of necking. Substituting the power law gives 500(0.3)εT−0.7 = 500εT0.3. Solving gives εT = 0.30. The true stress there is 500(0.30)0.3 ≈ 348.4 MPa.
  2. Calculate the engineering stress at that strain. At constant volume, A/A0 = e−εT. Therefore σTS = Fmax/A0 = σT(A/A0) = 500(0.30)0.3e−0.30 ≈ 258 MPa.
