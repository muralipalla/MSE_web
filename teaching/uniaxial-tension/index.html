01 / The measurements

Four ways to describe a pull

Let F be the tensile force, L0 the original gauge length, L the current gauge length, A0 the original cross-sectional area, and A the current area.

Original area

Engineering stress

σE = F / A0

Force divided by the initial area. It is the stress plotted in the conventional test curve.

Original length

Engineering strain

εE = (L − L0) / L0

Change in gauge length divided by the initial gauge length; strain is dimensionless.

Current length

True strain

εT = ln(L / L0)

Accumulated incremental extension relative to the current length.

Current area

True stress

σT = F / A

Force divided by the instantaneous cross-sectional area at the section considered.

Before necking: εT = ln(1 + εE); assuming uniform deformation and approximately constant volume, σT ≈ σE(1 + εE). After necking, measure the local neck area for local true stress; gauge-length strain is no longer a local strain.

02 / The onset of plasticity

Yield stress and proof stress

Yield stress

The stress at which appreciable permanent deformation begins. Some materials, such as certain low-carbon steels, show a distinct upper yield point followed by a drop to a lower yield point and a yield plateau.

0.2% proof stress

For a smooth transition, draw a line parallel to the initial elastic slope starting at engineering strain 0.002. Its intersection with the curve defines the 0.2% offset proof stress: an operational measure associated with 0.2% permanent strain under ideal elastic unloading.

03 / Loading and unloading

Separate total strain

At a point on a tensile curve, the strain recovered on unloading is elastic; the strain that remains is plastic.

εtotal = εelastic + εplastic

For linear elastic unloading with slope E: εelastic = σ / E and εplastic = εtotal − σ / E. Use consistent stress units. This is a small-strain approximation; the unloading line is assumed parallel to the initial elastic line.

Example: E = 200 GPa, σ = 400 MPa and εtotal = 0.012 give εelastic = 0.002 and εplastic = 0.010.

05 / Read the curve

Yield points and energy

An illustrative engineering stress–strain curve for a ductile material with a distinct yield-point drop. The elastic region is widened for clarity; the curve is not experimental data.

Modulus of resilience: elastic energy absorbed per unit volume up to the elastic limit. For linear elasticity, Ur ≈ σy2 / (2E).

The total area to fracture is the modulus of toughness, an energy per unit original volume (J/m3). A distinct upper/lower yield is not present in every material.

06 / Beyond yield

Power-law hardening

As plastic deformation proceeds, dislocation interactions can make further deformation harder. A common fit to the plastic flow region is the Hollomon law:

σT = K (εp,T)n

K is the strength coefficient and n is the strain-hardening exponent. Here εp,T is true plastic strain. On a log–log plot, n is the slope. The law is an empirical fit over a suitable plastic range, not a model for the elastic region or all materials.

In simplified necking derivations, elastic strain is neglected and total true strain is used in the power law; then the predicted true strain at uniform necking is approximately n.

07 / Localization

Why a neck forms

Uniform elongation

At first, deformation is distributed along the gauge length. The specimen strengthens as it hardens, but its cross-sectional area also decreases.

Localized deformation

When hardening can no longer compensate for the loss of area, the load reaches a maximum and a local neck develops in a typical ductile tensile specimen. Beyond this point the engineering stress falls, even though local true stress may continue to rise.

For ideal uniform, rate-independent plastic deformation with approximately constant volume, the Considère condition at necking is dσT/dεT = σT.

08 / Maximum load

Ultimate tensile strength

The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is the maximum engineering stress reached during the test:

UTS = Fmax / A0

For a typical ductile metal, the maximum load marks the onset of necking. The fracture stress is the engineering stress at separation and may be lower than UTS. Brittle materials may fracture without a significant plastic region or visible neck.

09 / A related measurement

Brinell vs. Rockwell hardness

Both measure resistance to indentation, but they use different observations and test scales.

FeatureBrinellRockwell
IndenterTungsten carbide ball of specified diameterDiamond cone or carbide ball, depending on scale
ReadingMeasure indentation diameter optically; calculate HBW from force and curved indentation area.Measure permanent indentation depth under the specified preliminary force after the additional force is removed; the machine reports hardness directly.
Typical useRelatively large indent averages over a larger area; useful for coarse or heterogeneous materials.Fast direct readings; choose a scale suitable for material and thickness, for example HRB or HRC.
ReportState HBW and test conditions (ball diameter, force and dwell when applicable).State the scale, for example 60 HRC; different scales are not interchangeable.

Hardness can correlate with tensile strength for some materials, but conversion depends on material and test conditions. Use an appropriate conversion standard; do not treat hardness as a direct tensile measurement.
